This report specifies a complete e-commerce information system for an stationery online retailer. The proposed system supports customer accounts, product and category management, product browsing and search, shopping carts, checkout, payment, inventory, delivery tracking, reviews, promotions, administration, and management reporting. The report follows the information-system lifecycle from requirements determination through analysis, AI-enabled e-commerce opportunities, design, and code. AI functions are treated as supporting services within a dependable transactional platform, not as a replacement for the core e-commerce processes. Chapter 1 Requirements Determination 1.1 Business Background 1.1.1 Overview Mosa is a medium-sized online stationery retailer that serves customers through a web storefront and a mobile-responsive customer interface. The business sells pens, pencils, notebooks, paper, school supplies, files, folders, and office accessories. Its value proposition is to provide customers with reliable product information, transparent pricing, convenient ordering, secure payment options, and predictable delivery. The retailer competes through catalogue quality, product availability, service responsiveness, promotions, and the ability to turn a customer visit into a completed and well-supported order. The company currently operates with information distributed across spreadsheets, staff messages, and separate partner portals. Product records can be edited by several people, inventory changes may be recorded after the physical movement occurs, and staff must often reconcile order, payment, and shipping information manually. This creates a clear need for an integrated e-commerce information system. The proposed system is not merely a website: it is the operational platform that connects customer-facing shopping activities with catalogue administration, inventory control, order processing, payment, fulfilment, delivery, customer service, and management reporting. This chapter investigates the organisation before prescribing a technical solution. It explains the business context, identifies stakeholders and their information needs, analyses the information managed by the business, examines current problems and their consequences, and defines the purpose, boundary, information flows, and objectives of the proposed system. The findings are used directly in Chapter 2 for functional analysis, in Chapter 3 for AI-enabled opportunities, in Chapter 4 for architecture and data design, and in Chapter 5 for implementation planning. 1.1.2 Business Model

=== PAGE 6 ===
Mosa operates as a business-to-consumer retailer. Customers explore the online catalogue, compare available products, place selected items in a shopping cart, submit delivery and payment information, and receive an order confirmation. The retailer earns revenue from the difference between selling price and the cost of goods, while promotions may be used to stimulate demand, clear inventory, or encourage repeat purchase. Product availability, price, service quality, and delivery reliability directly influence conversion, repeat purchase, and customer trust. The business model contains two connected value streams. The first is the customer value stream: product discovery, evaluation, purchase, payment, delivery, and after-sales support. The second is the operational value stream: catalogue maintenance, inventory replenishment, stock allocation, order fulfilment, partner coordination, reporting, and performance improvement. The information system must serve both streams using the same authoritative data rather than allowing each department to maintain incompatible copies. Business Element Mosa Approach Information-System Need Sales channel Web and mobile online storefront. Consistent catalogue, responsive customer journey, and secure customer sessions. Revenue model Retail sales, campaign promotions, and cross-selling. Accurate prices, discount rules, order totals, and sales reporting. Order model Customer order is fulfilled from available stock and delivered by a shipping provider. End-to-end order status, inventory reservation, payment result, and tracking data. Partner model Payment and shipping providers exchange transaction and status data. Controlled integrations, audit records, and failure handling. Customer relationship Self-service shopping supplemented by staff support. Customer profiles, order history, notifications, reviews, and service cases. The system should therefore be designed around repeatable business processes rather than around individual screens. A product page is useful only when it is supported by reliable product, price, and stock data. Likewise, a successful payment is useful only when it produces one confirmed order, an auditable inventory movement, and a clear path to fulfilment and delivery. 1.1.3 Products and Customers The stationery catalogue contains products with unique product identifiers (product_id), names, categories, descriptions, images, prices, supplier references, promotion eligibility, selling units, and stock quantities. Relevant attributes depend on the product category: ink colour and tip size for pens; paper size and weight for paper; and page count and ruling for notebooks. Selling units may include an individual item, box, pack, or ream, and the catalogue must state the quantity contained in each unit. Mosa serves individual stationery buyers, including students, teachers, and people purchasing supplies for personal study or office use. Customers may register or, where policy permits, shop as guests. They may arrive through direct search, social campaigns, paid advertisements, email promotions, or repeat visits. Their needs differ: a first-time visitor needs clear discovery and reassurance; a returning customer needs efficient reordering and trustworthy order history; a customer with a problem needs prompt service and an unambiguous status. The proposed system must support these situations without exposing personal data or allowing one customer to access another customer’s information. Chapter 2 Requirements Analysis 2.1 Stakeholder Needs This section translates the Mosa business context, stakeholders, information assets, problems, and objectives in Chapter 1 into outcomes expected from the system. The needs are expressed independently of screen layout or implementation technology. They provide the rationale for the requirements in Section 2.2 and for the use cases later in this chapter. Several needs cross organizational boundaries: accurate inventory supports customers, warehouse staff, and managers, while a coherent order record supports customers, sales staff, and external service coordination. 2.1.1 Stakeholder Objectives Customers need to discover suitable products, compare current descriptions and prices, understand whether an item appears available, and complete an order with confidence. A visitor should be able to browse and search without providing unnecessary personal information. A registered customer needs an account view for personal order history and delivery status. After placing an order, the customer needs a clear distinction between order acceptance, payment processing, fulfilment, and shipment; these are different business events and must not be collapsed into one ambiguous “complete” state.

=== PAGE 17 ===
Administrators need to maintain product, category, price, attribute, and promotion data from a controlled source. They need validation that prevents incomplete or inconsistent information from being published. The system should make the current active record available consistently to public product views and internal staff views, while retaining an attributable history of significant updates. Sales staff need to respond to questions using the order and customer context required for service. They need to identify the current state, see relevant status history, and record unresolved issues for follow-up. The system should reduce manual reconciliation and make it clear whether a case can be resolved by Mosa or needs an external payment or shipping provider. Warehouse staff need to receive, adjust, pick, pack, and dispatch stock using product and quantity information that can be trusted. Their system actions must correspond to physical or approved operational events. They need a traceable record for receipts, reservations, fulfilment, and corrections so that stock discrepancies can be investigated rather than hidden by overwriting a balance. Managers need timely views of sales, inventory, order, promotion, and service activity. They need to know the selected period and status definitions behind a report so they can interpret results consistently. They need to identify exceptions and trends without manually combining disconnected records. The payment provider needs to receive an identifiable payment request and return an outcome that Mosa can link to the correct order. The provider remains responsible for payment processing. Mosa must not treat a request as paid until a valid result is received and associated with its transaction reference. The shipping provider needs accurate delivery information for an eligible order and a way to return a shipment reference and tracking events. The provider remains responsible for physical carriage. Mosa needs to associate each returned event with the corresponding order and communicate only the latest status actually known. Suppliers need a clear replenishment or receipt exchange where Mosa supports that workflow. If a direct system integration is unavailable, staff still need to record the source, product, and quantity of a receipt. Supplier records must remain distinguishable from customer orders. Shell 5.5 Deployment and Evolution The current application is intended for local demonstration and project evaluation. It runs without Docker or a separate database server. The documented prerequisites are Python 3.12 or later and Node.js 20.19 or later in the Node 20 line, or Node.js 22.12 or later. On Windows, the application is prepared and started from the project directory using: .\run.ps1 The script creates a Python virtual environment when necessary, installs Python dependencies, installs frontend dependencies when absent, builds the React application, and starts Flask. The application is available at http://127.0.0.1:5000. Flask serves both the API and the built frontend. SQLite data is stored in instance/mosa.sqlite by default and persists between application runs. The database path can be configured through the DATABASE environment variable. Other configuration options include SECRET_KEY, DEMO_MODE, and COOKIE_SECURE. Without a configured secret key, a new key is generated at startup and existing sessions become invalid after a restart. A backup utility is provided in scripts/backup.py. It creates a separate SQLite backup and performs integrity and foreign-key checks. Restoration should be performed while the application is stopped and should use a new destination file. Formal backup frequency, retention, and recovery targets remain to be defined. Future production deployment should introduce a production WSGI server, HTTPS, managed secrets, versioned database migrations, operational monitoring, and tested rollback procedures. Real payment integration will require provider-specific authentication, verified callbacks, reconciliation, and retry policies. Reliable asynchronous processing will require a durable job or event mechanism and workers; these are not present in the current prototype. Search can evolve by replacing the current colour-and-layout descriptor with an evaluated embedding model behind the existing adapter boundary. Evaluation should use representative stationery images and queries while preserving explicit filters and a text-search fallback. Further development should define reservation expiry, refund and return approval, data retention, and provider retry limits. The current prototype supports guest browsing and carts but requires authentication for checkout. It records support requests for refund-related issues without automatically transferring money. These boundaries should remain explicit when planning subsequent releases. Conclusion The proposed Mosa information system connects product discovery, ordering, payment, inventory, fulfilment and reporting through defined responsibilities and information flows. The analysis identifies stakeholder needs and validation issues; the design describes how those needs may be implemented while preserving distinct transactional states.
